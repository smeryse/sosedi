# Integrated Brand Storytelling, Reels Optimization, and Social Listening Playbook

The three systems should reinforce each other:

1. **Brand storytelling** defines what your brand means.
2. **Instagram Reels** turns that meaning into discoverable content.
3. **Social listening** shows whether the audience understood, believed, and emotionally connected with it.

Examples below are adapted to **Сожители**, the roommate-matching and shared-rental platform, but the frameworks apply to most consumer and B2B brands.

---

# 1. Brand authenticity storytelling framework

## The central principle

Authenticity is not created by sounding informal, emotional, or “human.”

A brand feels authentic when there is consistency between:

* what it claims;
* what it repeatedly does;
* what customers actually experience;
* what it is willing to admit;
* what evidence it provides.

Your story should therefore be designed as a **system of verifiable meaning**, not a single campaign slogan.

## The ROOTS framework

### R — Reality

Begin with a real human tension, not with your product.

A weak opening:

> Finding a roommate is difficult, so we created an innovative AI platform.

A stronger opening:

> People often spend more time checking whether a stranger is safe to live with than inspecting the apartment itself.

The reality should contain:

* a recognisable situation;
* an emotional cost;
* an unresolved contradiction;
* a consequence of doing nothing.

For Сожители, the underlying reality is not simply “housing is expensive.” It is:

> Better housing becomes affordable when people share it, but sharing a home requires trusting strangers with your money, safety, sleep, possessions, and personal boundaries.

That contradiction can support years of storytelling.

### Questions to answer

* What does the audience privately worry about?
* What are they embarrassed to admit?
* What does the industry pretend is not a problem?
* What does the audience currently tolerate because no better option exists?
* Where is the gap between the official process and lived reality?

---

### O — Origin

Explain why the brand had to exist.

An origin story should not be a corporate chronology:

> We were founded in 2026 by a multidisciplinary team.

It should explain the moment when an accepted industry assumption became intolerable.

A useful structure:

1. We observed something normalised.
2. It produced an unfair or absurd outcome.
3. Existing solutions treated the symptom.
4. We decided to change the underlying mechanism.
5. This became the brand’s operating belief.

Example:

> Rental platforms helped people find apartments, but once several strangers decided to rent together, the platforms disappeared. Payments, trust, household rules and replacing a departing roommate were left to private chats and verbal promises. Сожители was created to manage the part of shared renting that begins after people find one another.

The origin must connect directly to current product behaviour. Otherwise, it becomes mythology.

---

### O — Others are the protagonists

The brand should rarely be the hero.

A durable narrative usually has four roles:

| Narrative role | Function                            |
| -------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Audience       | Protagonist trying to make progress |
| Problem        | Obstacle or opposing system         |
| Brand          | Guide, organiser or enabler         |
| Product        | Tool that helps the protagonist act |

For Сожители:

* **Protagonist:** a student or young professional trying to afford a good home;
* **Obstacle:** fragmented trust, unsafe listings, informal agreements and unequal financial responsibility;
* **Guide:** Сожители;
* **Tool:** a verified group application, compatibility analysis and shared-rental operating system.

This prevents the common mistake of making every story about the company’s technology.

### Character selection

Use multiple protagonists rather than one generic customer:

* the newcomer who knows nobody in the city;
* the owner who fears being left with unpaid rent;
* the existing group whose third roommate suddenly leaves;
* the student who can afford a better district only through sharing;
* the quiet remote worker whose previous roommate held parties at night.

Each character reveals a different dimension of the same brand truth.

---

### T — Truth through evidence

Every important claim needs a corresponding form of proof.

| Claim                    | Appropriate proof                                 |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------- |
| “We understand renters”  | Interviews, quotes, field observation             |
| “Matching works”         | Match-to-conversation and match-to-move-in data   |
| “Owners save time”       | Time-to-lease comparison                          |
| “Groups are more stable” | Three- and six-month retention                    |
| “The platform is safer”  | Verification coverage and fraud-intervention data |
| “We care about fairness” | Explainable recommendations and appeal process    |

Dove’s Real Beauty platform became credible because it was sustained through representation commitments, research and self-esteem programmes—not merely through emotional advertising. Its current pledge includes using real women, avoiding digital distortion and not substituting AI-generated women for real representation. ([dove][1])

Patagonia’s Worn Wear story similarly connects narrative to operating behaviour: repair services, resale, trade-ins and stories about the lives of used garments. The brand is not merely telling customers to consume less; it provides infrastructure that helps products remain in use. ([Patagonia Worn Wear®][2])

### Build an evidence ledger

For every campaign, document:

* narrative claim;
* supporting data;
* customer example;
* operational behaviour;
* risk of exaggeration;
* proof visible to the public.

If no evidence exists, either weaken the claim or create the operational capability before publishing it.

---

### S — Serial expression

Strong storytelling is episodic.

Do not attempt to communicate the entire brand through one manifesto video. Build a repeatable narrative universe with recurring formats.

For Сожители:

| Series                      | Narrative function                           |
| --------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| “Before we moved in”        | Anxiety, expectations and first impressions  |
| “The question nobody asked” | Compatibility conflicts discovered too late  |
| “Roommate economics”        | Transparent rent and room-price distribution |
| “Owner’s side of the door”  | Owners explain their fears and criteria      |
| “One month later”           | Outcomes after matching                      |
| “Shared-home rules”         | Real household agreements                    |
| “When someone moves out”    | Replacement and deposit scenarios            |
| “Verified, not perfect”     | Honest explanation of safety limitations     |

Serial formats make the brand recognisable while allowing the individual stories to remain varied.

---

## The narrative spine

Use this seven-part structure for campaigns, films, case studies and founder presentations.

### 1. Familiar world

Show the audience’s current behaviour.

> Three students find a promising apartment, but the owner refuses because none of them wants to be responsible for the other two.

### 2. Hidden tension

Reveal what is really at stake.

> The owner does not dislike students. He dislikes uncertainty.

### 3. Inciting event

Something forces a decision.

> One member must move within ten days or lose a new job opportunity.

### 4. Attempts and friction

Show failed workarounds:

* Telegram chats;
* spreadsheets;
* verbal agreements;
* one person collecting all the money;
* unverified screenshots;
* awkward interrogations.

### 5. Change in understanding

The protagonist discovers that the problem is not “finding people.”

> The missing product is a trustworthy rental group.

### 6. Meaningful action

Show the product in use:

* identity verification;
* compatibility questions;
* combined budget;
* room-price allocation;
* group application;
* owner approval.

### 7. New reality

Demonstrate a specific transformation.

> The owner receives one structured application instead of three unrelated messages. The tenants know their individual payments, shared rules and replacement procedure before moving in.

---

## Emotional architecture

Select one primary emotional movement per story.

| Starting emotion  | Desired ending         |
| ----------------- | ---------------------- |
| Suspicion         | Informed trust         |
| Isolation         | Belonging              |
| Embarrassment     | Normalisation          |
| Chaos             | Control                |
| Financial anxiety | Achievable possibility |
| Vulnerability     | Protected agency       |
| Conflict          | Fair resolution        |

Do not try to create happiness, trust, inspiration, urgency and humour simultaneously. Emotional precision is more convincing than emotional volume.

---

## Authenticity safeguards

Before publishing, run the story through five tests.

### The contradiction test

Would the audience find a product behaviour that contradicts the story?

A brand cannot campaign for transparent matching while hiding why candidates were ranked.

### The specificity test

Could a competitor replace your logo and publish the same story?

“Home is where we belong” is generic.
“Know the payment rules before learning your roommate’s Wi-Fi password” is distinctive.

### The vulnerability test

Does the story admit a real limitation?

For example:

> Compatibility analysis cannot guarantee friendship. It identifies practical issues that people should discuss before signing.

### The customer dignity test

Does the customer appear capable, or are they presented as helpless until rescued by the brand?

### The continuity test

Can the organisation behave consistently with this narrative for several years?

---

## Corporate storytelling examples

### Dove — Real Beauty

**Mechanism:** challenge an industry convention, centre real people, support the position with long-term commitments and research.

**Lesson:** authentic storytelling becomes powerful when the brand makes a public constraint on its own behaviour. Dove’s narrative is stronger because it commits the brand to representation standards rather than leaving “real beauty” as a vague creative theme. ([dove][1])

### Patagonia — Worn Wear

**Mechanism:** transform used products into carriers of personal history and make repair part of the customer experience.

**Lesson:** the most believable purpose story is embedded in the business model. Worn Wear supports trade-ins, resale and repair rather than merely producing sustainability-themed advertising. ([Patagonia Worn Wear®][2])

### Airbnb — Belong Anywhere

**Mechanism:** turn accommodation into a story about acceptance, hosts and human connection.

**Lesson:** a brand platform can unite product, community and public commitments. Airbnb has repeatedly tied its mission to belonging, while its community stories give that abstract idea human protagonists. ([Airbnb Newsroom][3])

# 2. Beginner’s technical manual for Instagram Reels optimization

## How the Reels algorithm works

Instagram does not operate through one universal algorithm. Different surfaces—Feed, Explore, Stories and Reels—use different ranking systems and predictions.

For Reels, Instagram evaluates signals to predict whether a person is likely to:

* watch;
* continue watching;
* like;
* comment;
* share or reshare;
* interact with the creator;
* find the content relevant to their interests.

Signals include a viewer’s past activity, information about the Reel, information about the account and prior interaction between the viewer and creator. The exact weighting is personalised and changes over time. ([about.instagram.com][4])

The practical implication is:

> Instagram does not primarily reward a Reel because it contains a particular hashtag, editing style or posting-time trick. It distributes a Reel more widely when predicted viewers respond positively to it.

Reels can be recommended to people who do not follow the creator, making them an important discovery format. Instagram also introduced Trial Reels, which allow eligible creators to test content with non-followers before deciding whether to share it more broadly. ([Instagram][5])

---

## Step 1: Confirm recommendation eligibility

Before optimising individual videos:

1. Use a professional Creator or Business account.
2. Open **Account Status**.
3. Check whether content or profile elements restrict recommendation eligibility.
4. Review Instagram’s Recommendation Guidelines.
5. Resolve policy issues before increasing production volume.

A Reel may remain visible to followers while being unsuitable for broader recommendation. Instagram explicitly distinguishes removal from reduced recommendation or distribution of problematic content. ([Transparency Center][6])

---

## Step 2: Define a narrow audience promise

The algorithm needs repeated behavioural evidence about who values your content.

Do not begin with:

> Our audience is anyone interested in housing.

Use:

> Russian students and young professionals who are considering renting a flat with people they do not already know.

Then define three content jobs:

1. **Recognition:** “This is exactly my problem.”
2. **Utility:** “This helps me make a better decision.”
3. **Proof:** “This product or creator understands the real situation.”

For Сожители:

* recognition: roommate horror stories;
* utility: questions to ask before moving in;
* proof: real compatibility breakdowns and successful group formation.

---

## Step 3: Build content pillars

Use three to five recurring pillars.

| Pillar                | Example Reel                                              |
| --------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Conflict prevention   | “Five questions to ask before choosing a roommate”        |
| Rental economics      | “Why equal rent is not always fair”                       |
| Safety                | “Three signs the room listing may be fake”                |
| Social proof          | “How three strangers formed a rental group in seven days” |
| Product demonstration | “What an 86% compatibility result actually means”         |

A content pillar should be broad enough for at least 20 ideas but narrow enough to attract the same audience repeatedly.

---

## Step 4: Script for retention

Use the **HVP3 structure**:

### H — Hook

Create immediate relevance in the first sentence or visual moment.

Weak:

> Today we will discuss choosing roommates.

Stronger:

> Your future roommate’s job matters less than what they do with dirty dishes.

### V — Value promise

Tell the viewer why they should continue.

> Here are the three questions that reveal more than a 20-minute interview.

### P1 — Progression

Reveal information in a logical sequence.

1. Money.
2. Guests.
3. Cleaning.
4. Exit conditions.

### P2 — Proof

Include:

* an example;
* a screenshot;
* a customer quote;
* an observed pattern;
* a mini-demonstration.

### P3 — Payoff

End with a useful conclusion—not merely “follow for more.”

> If you disagree on guests and early departure, a high lifestyle-match score will not save the arrangement.

### Prompt

Ask for a natural action:

> Which question would you add?

Avoid forcing unrelated engagement.

---

## Step 5: Design the opening frame

The opening should communicate three things without sound:

* topic;
* tension;
* reason to continue.

Example text:

> “91% compatible—until they discussed guests.”

Use:

* a clear face or recognisable scene;
* large readable text;
* movement immediately;
* visual contrast;
* no extended logo animation.

Instagram’s creator guidance emphasises capturing attention, length, reach, engagement and personalised best practices through its in-app education hub. ([Instagram][7])

---

## Step 6: Produce for mobile consumption

Recommended production practice:

* vertical 9:16 composition;
* important text away from interface edges;
* readable subtitles;
* clean speech or intentional sound;
* sufficiently bright, sharp footage;
* quick removal of pauses that add no meaning;
* one primary idea per Reel.

Instagram publishes dedicated guidance for maintaining video quality and export quality. It has also long advised creators to avoid visibly recycled content, particularly content carrying another platform’s watermark. ([Instagram][8])

Do not confuse fast editing with retention. A slower clip can perform well if tension, curiosity or usefulness remains high.

---

## Step 7: Package the Reel accurately

### Cover

Communicate the benefit:

* “7 questions before moving in”
* “How to split unequal rooms”
* “A safe group rental in 60 seconds”

### Caption

Use the caption to add context, not repeat the full video.

Suggested structure:

1. one-sentence summary;
2. practical clarification;
3. one question or next step.

### Keywords

Use natural words in:

* spoken audio;
* on-screen text;
* caption;
* profile description.

This helps both human comprehension and content classification.

### Hashtags

Use a small relevant set rather than a cloud of unrelated high-volume tags:

* category;
* audience;
* location;
* problem.

Example:

`#совместнаяаренда #снятьквартиру #сожители #арендакраснодар`

Hashtags are metadata, not a substitute for audience response.

---

## Step 8: Publish and distribute

After posting:

1. Respond to substantive comments.
2. Share the Reel to Stories with additional context.
3. Send it only to people or communities for whom it is genuinely relevant.
4. Avoid artificial engagement exchanges.
5. Pin useful comments that improve the content.
6. Record performance after 24 hours, 72 hours and seven days.

Instagram introduced reposts in August 2025; a public Reel reposted by another user may be recommended to that person’s followers, providing an additional route to discovery. ([Facebook][9])

---

## Step 9: Measure what matters

Do not optimise for views alone.

### Core metrics

#### Hold rate

If your dashboard provides three-second views:

[
\text{Hold Rate}=\frac{\text{3-second views}}{\text{Plays}}\times100
]

Shows whether the opening stopped viewers.

#### Average watch percentage

[
\text{Average Watch %}=
\frac{\text{Average watch time}}{\text{Video duration}}\times100
]

#### Completion rate

[
\text{Completion Rate}=
\frac{\text{Completed views}}{\text{Plays}}\times100
]

#### Share rate

[
\text{Share Rate}=
\frac{\text{Shares}}{\text{Accounts reached}}\times100
]

#### Save rate

[
\text{Save Rate}=
\frac{\text{Saves}}{\text{Accounts reached}}\times100
]

#### Follow conversion

[
\text{Follows per 1,000 views}=
\frac{\text{Follows attributed to Reel}}{\text{Views}}\times1000
]

#### Profile conversion

[
\text{Profile Conversion}=
\frac{\text{Profile visits}}{\text{Accounts reached}}\times100
]

Interpret metrics together:

| Pattern                          | Likely diagnosis                                          |
| -------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| High reach, low watch time       | Hook or audience mismatch                                 |
| Good watch time, few shares      | Entertaining but not socially useful                      |
| High saves, low follows          | Useful isolated topic, weak account promise               |
| High profile visits, low follows | Profile positioning is unclear                            |
| Low reach, strong ratios         | Good content with limited initial testing; repeat concept |
| Many comments, poor sentiment    | Controversy, not necessarily healthy engagement           |

---

## Step 10: Run controlled tests

Do not change the topic, hook, length, visual style and call to action simultaneously.

Test one variable at a time.

### Example experiment

**Hypothesis:** conflict-based openings improve first-three-second retention.

* Version A: “Three questions to ask a roommate.”
* Version B: “This one question could prevent six months of conflict.”

Keep the body, speaker, length and publication window similar.

Trial Reels can be useful for testing formats with non-followers before wider sharing. ([Instagram][10])

---

## Four-week beginner plan

### Week 1: Baseline

Publish three Reels using different pillars. Record:

* reach;
* average watch time;
* shares;
* saves;
* profile visits;
* follows.

### Week 2: Hook testing

Create three variations of the best-performing topic with different openings.

### Week 3: Format testing

Compare:

* direct-to-camera;
* story reenactment;
* screen demonstration;
* interview;
* list format.

### Week 4: Conversion

Create content that connects the problem to a next step:

* compatibility quiz;
* checklist;
* waiting list;
* profile creation;
* apartment-group application.

Keep a test log:

| Date | Topic | Hook | Format | Length | Reach | Watch % | Shares | Saves | Follows | Learning |
| ---- | ----- | ---- | ------ | -----: | ----: | ------: | -----: | ----: | ------: | -------- |

# 3. Advanced social-listening and brand-sentiment template

## Objective

A useful sentiment system must answer more than:

> Are mentions positive or negative?

It should reveal:

* why sentiment changed;
* which audience changed;
* what topic caused it;
* how rapidly it is spreading;
* whether the change affects consideration, trust or purchase;
* whether the brand response improved the situation.

---

## Section A: Research definition

### 1. Business question

Examples:

* Is trust increasing after the verification campaign?
* Which concerns prevent owners from listing apartments?
* What causes negative sentiment during onboarding?
* Are people discussing Сожители as a rental service, dating service or safety service?
* Does sentiment differ between renters and owners?

### 2. Scope

Define:

| Field               | Example                                                  |
| ------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Brand               | Сожители                                                 |
| Markets             | Russia; Krasnodar and Adygea                             |
| Languages           | Russian, English                                         |
| Period              | Rolling 90 days                                          |
| Platforms           | VK, Telegram, Instagram, YouTube, Reddit, reviews, media |
| Competitors         | Avito, ЦИАН, roommate groups, coliving operators         |
| Audience            | Renters, owners, developers, agents                      |
| Reporting frequency | Daily alerts, weekly review, monthly analysis            |

---

---

## Section C: Sentiment categorisation

### Polarity

Use five categories rather than three:

* strongly positive;
* positive;
* neutral;
* negative;
* strongly negative.

Add **mixed** when a single mention includes meaningful positive and negative components.

Example:

> “The matching questions are excellent, but asking for passport verification before I see any useful profiles feels excessive.”

This is:

* positive on matching;
* negative on onboarding;
* anxious emotion;
* evaluation-stage feedback.

A single overall “neutral” label would destroy the insight.

---

## Topic-level sentiment

One mention may contain several topic sentiments.

| Mention                                        | Matching | Verification |    Price |  Support |
| ---------------------------------------------- | -------: | -----------: | -------: | -------: |
| “Great matches, but verification is intrusive” | Positive |     Negative |        — |        — |
| “Support solved the deposit problem quickly”   |        — |            — | Positive | Positive |
| “Cheap, but profiles look fake”                | Negative |     Negative | Positive |        — |

Store sentiment at both:

* mention level;
* aspect or topic level.

---

## Emotion layer

Polarity describes direction. Emotion explains the psychological state.

Recommended categories:

* trust;
* relief;
* belonging;
* excitement;
* confusion;
* disappointment;
* frustration;
* anger;
* fear;
* disgust;
* embarrassment.

For a shared-rental product, **fear and trust** may be strategically more important than generic positivity.

---

## Intent layer

Classify what the person is doing:

* recommending;
* comparing;
* asking for help;
* reporting a defect;
* warning others;
* considering purchase;
* requesting a feature;
* defending the brand;
* joking or participating in a meme;
* organising collective action.

A negative question is different from a negative warning.

---

## Section D: Core KPIs

### 1. Net sentiment score

[
\text{Net Sentiment}=
\frac{\text{Positive}-\text{Negative}}
{\text{Positive}+\text{Neutral}+\text{Negative}}\times100
]

Range: −100 to +100.

Always report sample size beside it.

---

### 2. Negative mention rate

[
\text{Negative Mention Rate}=
\frac{\text{Negative mentions}}
{\text{All relevant mentions}}\times100
]

Useful for comparing periods with different total volume.

---

### 3. Share of voice

[
\text{Share of Voice}=
\frac{\text{Brand mentions}}
{\text{All category-brand mentions}}\times100
]

Calculate both:

* total share of voice;
* positive share of voice;
* negative share of voice.

High share of voice caused by a scandal is not success.

---

### 4. Engagement-weighted sentiment

Assign values:

* strongly positive = +2;
* positive = +1;
* neutral = 0;
* negative = −1;
* strongly negative = −2.

[
\text{Weighted Sentiment}=
\frac{\sum(\text{Sentiment value}\times\text{Impact weight})}
{\sum\text{Impact weights}}
]

Impact weight can combine:

* reach;
* views;
* shares;
* comments;
* author relevance.

Cap the weight of extremely large accounts so one viral mention does not erase the broader conversation.

---

### 5. Issue velocity

[
\text{Issue Velocity}=
\frac{\text{Mentions in current window}}
{\text{Average mentions in baseline windows}}
]

Example:

* normal verification complaints: 8 per day;
* current day: 40;
* issue velocity: 5×.

Use this for alerts.

---

### 6. Sentiment velocity

[
\text{Sentiment Velocity}=
\text{Current Net Sentiment}-\text{Baseline Net Sentiment}
]

A rapid fall from +20 to −15 is more actionable than a stable score of −5.

---

### 7. Complaint concentration

[
\text{Top Topic Concentration}=
\frac{\text{Negative mentions in largest topic}}
{\text{All negative mentions}}\times100
]

If 62% of negativity concerns document verification, the organisation has one concentrated issue rather than broad rejection.

---

### 8. Response rate

[
\text{Response Rate}=
\frac{\text{Actionable mentions receiving a response}}
{\text{All actionable mentions}}\times100
]

### 9. Median first-response time

Use the median, not only the average, because a few extreme cases distort the mean.

### 10. Resolution rate

[
\text{Resolution Rate}=
\frac{\text{Resolved cases}}
{\text{Cases requiring resolution}}\times100
]

### 11. Sentiment recovery

[
\text{Recovery Rate}=
\frac{\text{Negative cases becoming neutral or positive}}
{\text{Negative cases receiving a response}}\times100
]

This connects community management to actual perception change.

---

### 12. Advocacy rate

[
\text{Advocacy Rate}=
\frac{\text{Organic recommendations and defences}}
{\text{All relevant mentions}}\times100
]

Examples:

* “I used it and the verification was worth it.”
* “They found us a replacement roommate in three days.”
* users correcting misinformation without being prompted.

---

### 13. Purchase-intent rate

[
\text{Purchase Intent Rate}=
\frac{\text{Mentions expressing trial or purchase intent}}
{\text{Relevant consideration-stage mentions}}\times100
]

For Сожители:

* “Where can I sign up?”
* “Is this available in Krasnodar?”
* “Can owners list apartments?”
* “How much does verification cost?”

---

## Section F: Quality assurance

### Human-coded validation sample

Each month:

1. Randomly select 200–500 mentions.
2. Have two analysts classify them independently.
3. Compare agreement.
4. Review sarcasm, mixed sentiment and ambiguous terms.
5. Update taxonomy and model prompts.

Track:

* precision;
* recall;
* F1 score;
* inter-annotator agreement;
* uncategorised rate.

### Special errors to monitor

* sarcasm;
* quoted criticism;
* news headlines;
* brand name ambiguity;
* bot-generated repetition;
* slang;
* negation;
* multilingual switching;
* screenshots containing text;
* video sentiment not represented in captions.

# Final operating model

Run the three systems as a monthly loop:

1. **Listen:** identify audience tensions and emotional language.
2. **Choose:** select one tension that aligns with the brand’s operating truth.
3. **Build:** create a narrative with a protagonist, conflict, proof and transformation.
4. **Distribute:** adapt the narrative into Reels and other serial formats.
5. **Measure:** track retention, sharing, sentiment, intent and topic-level response.
6. **Learn:** determine whether the audience believed the story.
7. **Change:** improve either the storytelling or the underlying customer experience.

The most important rule is:

> When storytelling and sentiment data conflict, investigate the product experience before rewriting the message.

A brand cannot content-strategy its way out of an operational contradiction.
